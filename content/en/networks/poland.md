## Why Poland is a price comparison with one coverage footnote

Poland is one of the easiest countries in Europe to buy mobile data well, and one of the easiest to get wrong. Four competitive national networks, data that costs less per gigabyte than almost anywhere in the Union, and full eSIM support across all four operators. On coverage there is very little to argue about.

The two things that catch people out are both procedural. Polish law requires every prepaid line to be registered at the point of sale, which contradicts the widespread belief that Poland is a no-registration market. And Warsaw Chopin, the airport most visitors arrive at, has no dedicated carrier counter at all.

Everything else is settled. All {{< count-providers >}} brands we track ride all four networks, so the host-network question disappears and the comparison becomes purely about price and allowance. Our [Poland plan ranking](/compare/poland/) runs that comparison, and the [allowance calculator](/tools/) sizes a plan to your dates.

## Registration is the rule and the airport counter is not the answer

The registration requirement is a legal obligation rather than a shop policy, and it applies to prepaid lines as well as contracts. Bring a passport or a European identity card to the point of sale and the clerk records it, usually in a few minutes. A tax number is not required for a prepaid line.

Where you complete it matters more than it should. Krakow airport has shops from two of the four networks in arrivals, so you can buy a specific network and have the registration done properly. Warsaw Chopin does not: what is available there is convenience-store stock, which means the SIM is sold rather than set up, and there is no one behind the till whose job is mobile plans.

That asymmetry is the practical argument for a travel eSIM in Poland. It is not that the local SIM is expensive or hard to register — it is that the quality of the local experience depends on which airport you flew into.

## Which Polish network to ride

Poland is unusual in our data in that four networks compete rather than three, and the speed spread between them is wide even though the coverage argument is not.

### T-Mobile won the speed award and the most awards overall

T-Mobile won Download Speed outright at 90 Mbps in Opensignal's May 2026 report and was the most awarded operator with nine awards. It also has the widest 5G footprint of the four, which is the reason its download figure pulls clear of the rest. If speed is your priority, this is the host to look for.

### Orange took Consistent Quality and Reliability from it

Orange measured 81.3 Mbps on the same download award, second to T-Mobile but close enough that the gap is not decisive, and it overtook T-Mobile to win Consistent Quality and Reliability. Those two awards describe the experience rather than the peak, and Orange also carries the widest 4G coverage of the four, including rural areas, which is what makes it the better host for a driving trip.

### Play is the budget brand that grew up

Play measured 53.6 Mbps on the download award, third of the four, and it is the network whose reputation has changed most: it began as the budget option and is now a serious coverage player with a large 5G footprint of its own. It is also the network whose dead zones on the bus route south from Krakow to Zakopane are well known enough to be worth planning around.

### Plus is the fourth network and the least differentiated

Plus is the smallest of the four and competes on price and on the 2600 MHz band. Its measured numbers sit in the same band as Play and T-Mobile in our profiles, and there is no itinerary in Poland for which Plus is the specific answer.

Read the awards as a ranking and the speed bands as indicative ranges. One set comes from Opensignal's independent measurement programme, the ranges are our own compilation from carrier listings, and the specific mast you attach to still decides what you feel.

## Poland prepaid rules and why the local eSIM route is open

Two Polish rules shape how you buy data, and the second one is more permissive than in most of the region.

| | Travel eSIM | Polish prepaid SIM | Polish carrier eSIM |
|---|---|---|---|
| ID required | None | Passport or national ID at the point of sale | Yes, sometimes verified by selfie in an app |
| Where the registration happens | Before you fly | At the point of sale, in minutes | In store or digitally |
| Works across the European Union | Only with a regional plan | Yes, under roam-like-at-home | Yes, under roam-like-at-home |
| Best for | Almost every short trip | Longer stays wanting a local number | Longer stays that want an app-based product |

The registration picture is strict but quick. Poland registers prepaid lines against a passport or a national identity card at the point of sale, so a visitor needs the physical document, and the step cannot be skipped.

The eSIM picture is the permissive one. All four networks support eSIM as standard, and the app-based products complete identity verification digitally, which means a visitor who wants a Polish number has a workable route that does not involve a shop. That is different from the situation in Morocco or Vietnam, and it makes Poland a destination where the local eSIM is a real alternative rather than a dead end.

## 5G in Poland and where the countryside thins out

All four networks run commercial 5G and every Polish carrier profile we track lists it, with T-Mobile carrying the widest footprint and Orange the widest 4G reach. The 5G build is concentrated in the cities and along the main transit corridors, which is where most of a Polish itinerary sits anyway.

The gaps are in the periphery rather than the middle. The Bieszczady mountains in the south-east, the Masurian lake district in the north-east, the Baltic coast away from the Tricity, and the rural stretches between towns on secondary roads all thin out, and the bus route from Krakow south to Zakopane crosses pockets where not every network holds a signal.

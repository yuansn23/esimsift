## Why Egypt is a 4G country with a 5G badge

Egypt is not short of networks. It has three national operators with licensed 5G spectrum, every travel eSIM brand we track connects to all three, and Cairo delivers a perfectly usable mobile internet experience. The two things worth understanding before you buy are both about proportions rather than presence: how little of the country is actually on 5G, and how much paperwork sits in front of a local line.

Take 5G first. Egypt launched commercial 5G in 2025, and the first independent measurement we have describes a network you occasionally reach rather than one you stay on — Vodafone measured 9.6% time on 5G and 37.5% 5G availability in Opensignal's February 2026 report. Those are not disappointing numbers for a first year, but they do describe where 5G is: central Cairo, the larger resorts, the busiest business districts. The rest of the country runs on 4G, and in the desert and the Sinai interior it runs on less than that.

## The counter rule that decides how you buy data

The second thing is the registration rule, and it is a legal requirement rather than a shop policy.

Every prepaid line in Egypt has to be registered to a passport before it activates. The registration happens in person at an official store or carrier counter, where the document is recorded and a fingerprint is taken. Cairo airport has carrier counters that operate around the clock, and shops in the city handle the same process during business hours.

For a visitor that produces an unusual set of trade-offs. The local SIM is the cheap option and stays the cheap option, but it costs you a queue on arrival and it ties you to a counter for the initial purchase. A travel eSIM removes the counter entirely: the profile is provisioned before you fly, so the first thing your phone does on landing is connect.

There is a real cost on the other side, and it is worth being honest about it. An Egyptian number is occasionally useful — for a hotel callback, for a local booking, for a delivery arriving at the wrong address — and a data-only travel eSIM cannot give you one. If a local number matters to your trip, buy both: the eSIM to be online from the aircraft door, and a prepaid SIM once you have the time to stand in a queue.

## Which Egyptian network to ride

The three networks differ less than the awards table suggests inside the cities and rather more outside them, so the useful question is where your itinerary goes.

### WE leads the independent testing

WE is the consumer brand of Etisalat Misr, and it took the Best Network title in Opensignal's February 2026 Egypt report with seven outright wins, including Download Speed at 25.5 Mbps and the fastest 5G download at 75.8 Mbps. It is the network to prefer if your trip leaves the Nile valley, because the Sinai interior and the roads behind the Red Sea resorts are where it holds on and the others thin out.

### Vodafone owns the valley and the coast

Vodafone did not win the headline award but it won the award that matters most to a holiday: it retained 5G Coverage, and its coverage of the Nile valley and the Red Sea coast is the reason resort towns and cruise stops work as well as they do. Its measured 9.6% time on 5G and 37.5% 5G availability are also the clearest published picture of how young Egyptian 5G is.

### Orange is the third network and the cheapest entry

Orange measured a lower speed band than Vodafone in our carrier profiles and is usually the cheapest of the three to buy into locally. It is a perfectly reasonable host for a city break in Cairo or a stay in Alexandria, and less obviously the right choice for a desert or Sinai itinerary.

Read those as indicative bands rather than as a ranking you can bank on. One set comes from Opensignal's independent measurement programme, the speed ranges are our own compilation from carrier listings, and the congestion on the specific mast you attach to still decides what you feel. Neither tells you how fast a travel eSIM will run for you.

## Egypt prepaid rules and why the local eSIM route stalls

Two Egyptian rules shape how you actually buy data, and the second one is unusual.

| | Travel eSIM | Egyptian prepaid SIM | Egyptian carrier eSIM |
|---|---|---|---|
| ID required | None | Yes, passport recorded at the counter | Yes, with Egyptian documents |
| eSIM availability | Standard | Rare, counter flows | Yes, but counter activation |
| Live before you land | Yes | No | No |
| Best for | Almost every short trip | Longer stays wanting a local number | Residents |

The registration picture is stricter than in most of Europe and the Middle East, and it is a legal requirement rather than a store policy. Egypt registers prepaid lines against a passport, so a visitor needs the physical document and should not expect a photograph to be accepted.

The eSIM picture is the opposite of the usual one. Egyptian carriers do offer eSIM, but the flows assume Egyptian identity documents and a counter visit, which means a visitor cannot activate one remotely. For a tourist that effectively pushes the eSIM route through an international travel eSIM provider rather than a local network, the reverse of the advice that works in Thailand or Turkey.

## 5G in Egypt and where coverage actually stops

All three networks run commercial 5G, and every Egyptian carrier profile we track lists it. What is worth knowing is where it lives. Measured time on 5G in the single digits means the network you mostly use is 4G, so plan around 4G and treat 5G as a city bonus.

Where the gaps are is predictable once you picture the map. The Western Desert beyond the oases, the Eastern Desert between the Nile and the Red Sea, the Sinai interior behind Sharm El Sheikh, and the long road south from Aswan towards Abu Simbel all thin out or stop. Download offline maps before any of those, and keep an eye on which network your phone has chosen when you leave a town.

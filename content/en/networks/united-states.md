## Why a US eSIM is a price decision before it is a coverage decision

American coverage is national, and that is unusual enough to change how you shop. Three carriers run the masts — T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon — and every one of the {{< count-providers >}} travel eSIM brands we track buys capacity from all three. The brand table further down shows it without exception.

The consequence is blunt. No brand can sell you better coverage in the United States, because every brand is buying the same infrastructure. European visitors often arrive expecting the opposite, since at home a budget plan really can mean a weaker network. Here it does not, and an entire axis of comparison disappears.

What survives is the plan itself — how much data, across how many days, at what price per gigabyte, with what hotspot rules and what fair-use terms. Our [United States plan ranking](/compare/united-states/) sorts every plan we track on those numbers rather than on carrier logos, and the brand section below explains why that is the only comparison available.

## Which US network leads on speed and which holds a signal longest

Availability is near-identical across the three. Performance is not, and both measurement firms agree on the direction.

### T-Mobile owns US speed and consistency

Opensignal's July 2026 cycle gave T-Mobile 12 of 16 awards outright, including Download Speed at 192.5 Mbps — roughly 97 Mbps clear of second place — and Reliability at 942 points against AT&T's 939 and Verizon's 932. Ookla separately recorded a 275.6 Mbps median download for the first half of 2026.

### Verizon owns US 5G reach

Verizon holds the widest coverage footprint of the three, which is why it is the network to care about if your route runs through national parks or long rural stretches.

### AT&T is the US middle path

AT&T wins one category outright with Time on Network at 99.6%, against Verizon's 99.5% and T-Mobile's 99.2%. That figure measures how much of the time you actually hold a usable connection, and on a trip that mixes cities with long drives it can matter more than a peak-speed headline.

Treat the two columns as separate rankings rather than one blended score. One averages what real users actually experienced, the other takes the midpoint of speed tests people ran themselves. The scoreboard above keeps them in separate columns for that reason. Both are industry-standard benchmarks, and neither says anything about how fast a travel eSIM will run on your trip — a plan's ceiling is set by the host network, but what you actually get also depends on congestion, your handset and where you are standing.

## Does a US eSIM keep working in Canada and Mexico

Whether a United States eSIM keeps working across the Canadian and Mexican borders decides a lot of purchases, and the answer is a warning. A United States eSIM is provisioned for American networks and stops working when you cross the border. North America has no equivalent of the European Union's roam-like-at-home rules, so a US-only plan does not quietly follow you north or south.

| | United States eSIM | North America eSIM |
|---|---|---|
| Networks it rides | T-Mobile, AT&T, Verizon | The US three plus Canadian and Mexican carriers |
| Works at the border | No | Yes, in all three countries |
| Price per GB | Lower | Higher, because it covers three markets |
| Buy it when | Your trip stays inside the US | Your itinerary crosses into Canada or Mexico |

If your trip touches Niagara Falls, Vancouver, Tijuana or Cancún, the regional plan is the right purchase — the premium is smaller than buying a second eSIM mid-trip, and it removes a failure that tends to surface at the worst possible moment. Coverage on the other side of each border is a separate question, covered on our [Canada eSIM networks](/networks/canada/) and [Mexico eSIM network](/networks/mexico/) pages.

## A US eSIM against a prepaid data plan and home roaming

The United States offers three ways to get mobile data, and the trade-offs are unusually clean because the country has no registration requirement at all.

| | Travel eSIM | Local prepaid SIM | Roaming on your home SIM |
|---|---|---|---|
| ID or passport | Not required | Not required | Not required |
| When it starts working | On landing, any hour | Store or airport counter opening hours | On landing |
| Data allowance | Fixed, bought up front | Fixed or monthly, renewed locally | Usually a small daily cap |
| Cost for a two-week trip | Posted price, no surprises | Cheap per GB, plus a trip to a store | Highest of the three in most cases |
| Main drawback | Data only, no local number | Counter queue and a new number to share | Bill shock if the daily cap is exceeded |

Because there is no ID rule, a prepaid SIM is a genuinely competitive option for a long stay — weeks rather than days. For a one or two week trip the arithmetic usually favours a travel eSIM, since it is bought before departure and needs no counter visit. Check your own home tariff first, because some carriers include the United States in their plans and others charge heavily for it. ## Bands and VoLTE what your phone needs for US 5G

All three carriers run commercial 5G and every US carrier profile on this page lists it, so 5G is not a differentiator between brands either. T-Mobile's 192.5 Mbps download figure is a 5G-era number, which means the technology is doing real work rather than sitting on a spec sheet.

Two device checks decide whether you actually get it. The first is band support: T-Mobile leans on 600 MHz Band 71 for rural 5G, and phones sold outside North America sometimes lack it. The American carriers also use bands that European and Asian models do not always tune, and the FCC's spectrum allocations are what set that map. The second check is more serious. All three carriers completed their [3G shutdowns in 2022](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/3g-phase-out), so a handset without VoLTE support will not register on any of them, data included. An eSIM is a downloadable profile rather than a physical chip, and it inherits whatever your handset supports — the standard is maintained by the [GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/esim/), so the profile is portable but the radio is not.

Then there is the practical side of a country this size. City 5G is easy to find; the gaps appear on the long drives between them, where Verizon's coverage advantage and AT&T's Time on Network score start to outweigh any peak figure. Live discounts sit on the [current eSIM deals](/esim-deals/), and the full destination list is on the [eSIM network map](/networks/).

Two details are worth confirming before you pay. Where 5G is absent the phone falls back to 4G, which on American networks is still fast enough for maps and messaging. And upload throughput matters more than most travellers expect, because posting photos and joining video calls from the road is the commonest way a small allowance disappears.

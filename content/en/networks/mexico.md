## How the Mexican line registration rule affects visitors

Start here, because this rule catches travellers who did not check. Since 9 January 2026 every mobile line in Mexico has to be registered to an identified user. It covers prepaid and postpaid alike, across every carrier, and it applies to lines bought inside the country. Foreign visitors register with a valid passport — no Mexican tax number is required — and an unregistered line is suspended, leaving emergency calls only. The regulator that oversees the market is the [IFT](https://www.ift.org.mx/).

Where it gets interesting is the scope. The rule targets lines issued by Mexican carriers. A travel eSIM bought from an international provider is issued abroad and arrives in Mexico as inbound roaming, which places it outside the registration process entirely. That is not a loophole in any meaningful sense — it is how the rule is written — but it does mean the paperwork question answers itself if you buy before you fly.

The rest of the Mexican market is unusually concentrated, and that shapes everything else on this page.

## Why Telcel dominates the Mexican market

Telcel is not one of three comparable networks. It is the network, and the measurements say so without hedging.

Opensignal's October 2025 report shows Telcel sweeping all four speed awards, with Download Speed Experience at 46.0 Mbps against AT&T's 27.2 Mbps and a 5G download of 180.7 Mbps — more than three times the competition's. It also kept Reliability Experience at 863 points out of 1000, 96 points clear of AT&T, and won Coverage Experience. Ookla separately named it Mexico's Best Mobile Network for the second half of 2025, with a median 5G download of 212.68 Mbps.

That dominance has a practical shape. In Mexico City, Guadalajara, Monterrey and Cancún, all three carriers perform well and the difference is a matter of degree. Outside them — in smaller towns, on the roads between cities, and anywhere inland from a resort corridor — Telcel's footprint is the difference between a working phone and none. It is why the carrier identity of your plan matters more in Mexico than in most destinations.

## Why the gap between Telcel AT&T and Movistar is so wide

The gap between Telcel in first place and Movistar in third is the widest of any market in our network map.

### Telcel is the Mexican benchmark everywhere

Telcel is best on speed, reliability and coverage width. If your itinerary includes anything beyond a major city or a resort, this is the answer.

### AT&T Mexico is the city network

AT&T won the Availability award in the same Opensignal cycle, meaning it holds a connection more of the time than its rivals in the areas it covers. In Mexico City and Monterrey it is genuinely competitive. On the routes between cities it is not.

### Movistar is Mexico's value option

Movistar is a budget option that runs on another carrier's infrastructure rather than on its own masts, which is why it does not appear in the award tables beside the other two. It works as a cheap city choice and is not the pick for a longer trip.

The two columns come from different kinds of measurement and should never be subtracted from one another. What they tell you is which carrier each source saw as strongest and in which category, not what your phone will register. Your own speed also depends on congestion, your handset and the bands it supports.

## Where Mexican coverage thins outside the resort corridors

Mexico's tourism geography is spread out, which makes coverage planning part of trip planning.

| | What to expect | Which network matters |
|---|---|---|
| Mexico City, Guadalajara, Monterrey | Fast 5G on all three networks | Any |
| Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Los Cabos, Puerto Vallarta | Good coverage along the resort corridors | Any, with Telcel ahead inland |
| Colonial cities and smaller towns | Solid 4G, thinner 5G | Telcel |
| The roads between them | Long stretches where coverage thins sharply | Telcel |
| Inland rural areas away from the main routes | Patchy at best, sometimes absent | Telcel or offline maps |

The rule of thumb is that the further your trip moves from a resort corridor or a major city, the more the carrier underneath your plan matters. Since every brand we track offers Telcel, that check is about the plan listing rather than about which company you buy from. Download offline maps for any long drive regardless, because even Telcel has gaps.

Only AT&T Mexico has shut down its 3G network so far, with Movistar following, while Telcel still operates 3G across parts of the country. Bring a 4G or 5G handset with VoLTE and the question does not come up. An eSIM is a profile your phone downloads rather than a physical card, a standard maintained by the [GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/esim/), and it will run on whatever bands your handset supports. The [compatibility check](/guides/esim-compatibility-check/) covers specific models.

## Where Mexican 5G reaches and what to check on your data plan

All three Mexican carriers we track list 5G, so the technology is not a differentiator at the brand level. What differs is where it exists.

Telcel's 5G averaged 180.7 Mbps in Opensignal's October 2025 testing, which is real throughput rather than a marketing label. The footprint follows the big cities, the main resort corridors and the business districts. Step off those routes and the phone drops to 4G, which in Mexico is still fast enough for maps, messaging and streaming.

That makes 5G a poor reason to pay more here, and coverage a good reason to choose carefully. The useful checks before you buy are whether the plan lists Telcel, whether it covers the regions on your route, and whether the allowance matches the length of the trip.

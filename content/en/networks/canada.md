## Why Canadian mobile data costs so much

Most destination pages start with the network. Canada should start with the bill, because that is where the decision actually happens. The country pairs a population smaller than California's with a landmass larger than the whole European Union, and the cost of covering it lands on very few subscribers. The result is a domestic data price among the highest in the developed world — several times what a European visitor pays at home.

For a visitor that is not a footnote, it is the entire comparison. A Canadian prepaid SIM is expensive by international standards even when it looks cheap locally, and a travel eSIM bought before departure usually undercuts it while skipping the store visit. The per-gigabyte numbers on our [ranking of Canada eSIM plans](/compare/canada/) make the gap concrete.

The network question, meanwhile, is simpler here than almost anywhere. All {{< count-providers >}} brands we track ride the same three carriers — Bell, Rogers and Telus — so no provider can sell you better coverage. And two of the three go further than that: they share infrastructure outright.

## How the Bell and Telus network sharing works

Bell and Telus are separate companies with a joint radio access network across most of Canada. The practical consequence is that their coverage footprints are near-identical and Opensignal has reported them statistically tied on overall download speed, at 91.5 to 91.9 Mbps — a gap inside the confidence interval. Choosing between them is a question of price and plan shape, not of where you will get signal.

Rogers is the genuine alternative. It runs its own network, is strongest in the east and in downtown cores, and holds the fastest upload speeds in the country — 13.1 Mbps overall and 25.3 Mbps on 5G in Opensignal's August 2026 cycle. Upload is easy to ignore until you are sending photos from a trip, and Rogers is the network that delivers it.

So Canada is really a two-footprint market rather than a three-carrier one, both national. It also means the per-brand table below is not a coverage comparison at all. It is a price list.

## How Bell Rogers and Telus differ on 5G speed and reliability

Bell, Rogers and Telus separate on performance even where they agree on availability.

### Bell leads on Canadian 5G speed

Bell retained the 5G download lead at 173.6 Mbps in Opensignal's August 2026 report, and Ookla named it Canada's Best and Fastest 5G provider for the second half of 2025 at a 171.17 Mbps median 5G download. Notably, Ookla declined to name an overall Best Mobile Network winner, which tells you how close the market is outside the 5G category.

### Rogers leads on consistency and app experience

Rogers took the most awards in the same cycle at nine, led Application Experience, and shared both Reliability and Consistent Quality with Telus. Its upload advantage is real and unusual in a market where every carrier chases download figures.

### Telus is the balanced Canadian network

Telus won Best Network and took Download Speed outright at 91.8 Mbps, and it shares both consistency wins with Rogers. On a drive through western Canada, Telus and Bell are interchangeable because of the shared network.

One reading habit matters here. The two firms disagree on some categories because they do not measure the same thing — one samples how the average user experiences the network, the other takes the median of speed tests people ran themselves. Where they diverge, treat each as its own verdict. Neither is a promise about your own connection, because congestion, your handset and local conditions all move the number.

The CRTC, which regulates Canadian telecoms, publishes consumer guidance on [roaming charges and how they work](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/phone/mobile/trav.htm), and the Wireless Code it enforces sets the rules a Canadian provider must follow on billing. That matters less for a prepaid SIM than for a contract, but it is worth knowing that Canadian consumer protection is comparatively strong. On price, a travel eSIM is the simpler purchase. An eSIM profile is a downloadable standard rather than a chip, which is why it can be bought from abroad at all — a point the [GSMA](https://www.gsma.com/esim/) documents for anyone who wants the mechanics.

## Does a Canada eSIM work in the United States

Usually you need a North America plan, not a Canada-only one. A Canada eSIM is provisioned for Canadian networks and stops at the border, and there is no continental free-roaming arrangement. If your trip crosses into the United States — and a great many Canadian itineraries do — buy a regional plan and confirm the country list before paying. The carrier detail on the other side is on our [United States eSIM networks](/networks/united-states/) page.

That also changes the arithmetic. A North America plan costs more per gigabyte than a Canada-only one because it covers a larger market, so buy it only if the itinerary actually crosses. If your trip stays inside Canada, a single-country plan is cheaper and simpler, and the [allowance calculator](/tools/) will size it. Two closing checks. Spectrum licences in Canada are issued nationally, and the bands in use do not match the European plan, which is one more reason to confirm your handset. It is also worth knowing that Rogers began phasing out 3G in 2025 with Bell and Telus following in Manitoba, so bring a device that supports 4G and VoLTE rather than an older one — our [check your phone](/guides/esim-compatibility-check/) guide lists the models that are safe. Canada offers visitors no fair-use roaming cushion of the kind the European Union provides, so a plan that actually covers the country is the only safe purchase.

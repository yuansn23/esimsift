Most people land at JFK with a working phone and a dead plan. The map will not load, the rideshare app spins, and the first genuinely useful thing bought in New York City turns out to be a data plan. Roaming on a home SIM is the expensive option. A prepaid SIM from an airport counter is the slow one. An eSIM is neither, because it installs on the phone before the flight and switches on when the plane lands. If the concept is new to you, the [eSIM basics guide](/guides/what-is-an-esim/) covers what the chip actually is.

Flying in from the UK or Ireland? Check whether the home plan's roaming zone includes the USA before leaving. Some European tariffs do. The ones that do not send the bill after you are already home.

Finding a plan is not the problem. Our US price database tracks every prepaid plan sold into the country. The problem is that the cheapest plan on the page is rarely the cheapest plan for the trip, and the network you think you are choosing is the same one every other brand hands you.

## Why a New York week is harder on data than you think

New York burns data in ways that catch people out.

The subway runs underground for long stretches, so the phone spends half the day hunting for a signal and re-syncing whenever it finds one. Rideshare pickups move two blocks while you wait for them. Photo uploads queue up on hotel Wi-Fi that dies the moment a video starts. None of that is heavy use on its own. Stacked over seven days in Manhattan it adds up to more than a beach week.

Our sizing for US trips runs like this:

- **1GB** covers maps, messaging and email for a short city break.
- **3GB to 5GB** is a normal week of maps, mail, rideshare apps and social feeds.
- **10GB or more** is for streaming, hotspot sharing or a working trip.

For a full week in New York, five gigabytes is the honest figure. It is not the cheapest product in the database, but it is the one that gets you back to the airport without watching a counter tick down.

## Every brand runs on the same three networks

Read enough eSIM reviews and you will meet the claim that the choice of provider decides which American network you get. T-Mobile is cast as the fast one, [AT&T as the middle path](/networks/united-states/#att-is-the-us-middle-path), [Verizon as the reach leader](/networks/united-states/#verizon-owns-us-5g-reach).

In the data we track, that is not how it works. Every USA plan in our database lists the same three networks. There is exactly one network combination in the entire file, and it is T-Mobile, [AT&T](/networks/united-states/#scoreboard) and [Verizon](/networks/united-states/#carriers) in every single plan, across every brand we cover.

The brand changes the price, the app and the support channel. It does not change the towers underneath. So paying a premium for better coverage in New York buys nothing that a cheaper plan does not already include. Choose on price, validity and data allowance instead. If you want to know how the three networks behave around the city, [the US network report](/networks/united-states/) pulls the measured performance apart carrier by carrier and city by city. The underlying figures come from [Ookla's US 5G index](https://www.speedtest.net/global-index/united-states) and [RootMetrics' carrier scores](https://www.rootmetrics.com/en-US/rootscore/map).

One thing does vary by phone rather than by brand: the bands and voice support your handset needs for US 5G, which our [US band and VoLTE notes](/networks/united-states/#bands-and-volte-what-your-phone-needs-for-us-5g) lay out.

## Which eSIM to buy for a New York week

Without the network variable, the decision comes down to three numbers: how long you stay, how much you upload, and how much you are willing to pay for a brand you have heard of. The picks below are the cheapest plans in the database that actually cover the trip described, which is not the same as the cheapest plan overall. If you want one answer instead of a table, the cheapest eSIM for New York of any length is the 500MB day pack at $0.51, the cheapest eSIM for New York that carries a full week is the 5GB plan at $5.00, and the best eSIM for New York by rate per gigabyte is the 50GB bucket at $0.60 per gigabyte.

### Cheapest for a short city break

A three or four day trip with maps, messaging and Wi-Fi in the evenings does not need much. **Roami's 1GB plan with 7 days of validity at $1.99** is the lowest entry price in the entire US file. If that sells out, Roamic lists the same 1GB and 7 days at $2.00, and a 3GB plan with 14 days at $3.00.

### Best value for a normal week

This is where the arithmetic gets interesting. **Roamic's 5GB plan with 21 days of validity at $5.00** works out at $1.00 per gigabyte, a quarter of what the median plan in the database charges. If you want a longer window rather than a lower price, **Ubigi's 5GB with 30 days at $6.00** covers the same week and stays valid for the rest of the month. Paying more buys a longer validity period, not more data.

### Best for a heavy week or hotspot use

Streaming, video calls and sharing a laptop connection push a week past 10GB. **Roamic's 10GB plan with 30 days of validity at $8.00**, or $0.80 per gigabyte, is the cheapest plan that carries a heavy week and still has validity left over afterwards. Roami's 10GB with 7 days is $10.99 and Ubigi's is $12.00, so the 30-day version is both cheaper and more flexible.

### Best rate for a stay of a month or more

Past two weeks the arithmetic flips and the largest buckets win. **Nomad's 50GB plan with 30 days of validity at $30.00** is the best rate in the entire database at **$0.60 per gigabyte**, and it is the only plan in our file that reaches that figure. Roamic's 50GB is $36.00 and Ubigi's is $40.00. If 50GB is more than you will ever use, Roamic and Ubigi both sell 20GB with 30 days for $15.00, which is $0.75 per gigabyte.

### If you would rather use a familiar brand

The cheapest plans are not always from the most familiar names, and that is a legitimate reason to pay more. For the same 5GB and a week or more of validity, **Nomad charges $10.00**, **Airalo and aloSIM charge $12.50** and **Saily charges $13.99**. Every one of them runs on the same three networks as the $5.00 option, so the extra money buys brand recognition, an app you have used before and a support desk you can reach, not better coverage.

One rule applies whichever brand you pick. If the name is new to you, check the fair-use policy and the refund terms on the plan page before paying, because those two documents are where the cheap plans differ from the expensive ones.

## The real limits inside unlimited USA plans

A large share of the plans in our file are unlimited, and the word does very different amounts of work depending on the brand. We read the fair-use policy behind every one of them. The published daily full-speed allowances look like this:

- **Saily** gives 5GB per day, then around 1 Mbps. That is the most generous published threshold we track.
- **Airalo, Roamic and Jetpac** give 3GB per day, then 1 Mbps until the cycle resets.
- **Nomad** gives 2GB per day, then 1 Mbps.
- **aloSIM** gives 1.5GB to 2GB per day, then around 512 Kbps.
- **Yesim** states 500MB to 2GB per day depending on destination, printed on the plan page before purchase.
- **Ubigi** varies per plan and the spread is large, with two plans at the same price seen offering 15GB in total against 5GB per day.
- **Roami** publishes 30GB per day, yet its own pages disagree with each other, since destination pages and the USA page describe the throttle differently. Treat the figure as destination-dependent and read the plan page for the country you are buying.
- **Holafly** publishes no daily figure at all and says throttling is temporary, never longer than 24 hours. Independent testing in early 2026 measured the cut-off nearer 500MB to 1GB per day in most Western destinations.

That is the difference between an unlimited plan that carries a week of normal use and one that quietly drops you to a speed where a map still loads and nothing else does.

Price is not the only surprise. The cheapest unlimited plan that covers a full week is **Roamic's 7-day tier at $18.00**, and the cheapest 30-day unlimited tiers start at **$49.99 and run to $73.90**, which is several times what the equivalent metered plans cost. The largest single-brand unlimited ladder in the US file is Holafly's, running from 3 days to 30 days with no metered option at all, which makes [Holafly's USA plans](/compare/united-states/holafly/) the clearest example of why the daily figure has to be read before the price.

## An eSIM against a prepaid SIM and home roaming

Both alternatives cost more than they look like they do.

A prepaid SIM bought at the airport counter means a queue, an ID check, a card swap and a price that is set for people who have just landed. Home roaming means paying your own carrier's out-of-zone rate, or buying a daily pass that is billed whether or not you use the data. An eSIM removes the queue and the pass, because it is bought in advance at a published per-gigabyte price. Our [comparison of the three options](/networks/united-states/#a-us-esim-against-a-prepaid-data-plan-and-home-roaming) sets out where each one wins.

## If the trip continues into Canada or Mexico

A plan sold as United States covers the United States. Crossing into Canada or Mexico either stops the data or bills it at a different rate, and a New York trip that becomes a Toronto weekend is a common way to get caught out.

Two options work. Buy a North America or regional plan that lists all three countries from the start, which is usually the cheapest route if you know the itinerary before you fly. Or keep a US-only plan and add a second one for the neighbour, which is simpler if the dates are uncertain. Compare the [Canada plans](/compare/canada/) and the [Mexico plans](/compare/mexico/) before deciding, and check the country list on the plan page rather than the plan name.

## Your first hour in New York

Install before the flight, not at the gate. An eSIM profile needs a data connection to download, so do it at home on Wi-Fi and let the plan activate itself when the phone registers in the USA. How the profile arrives differs by brand, with some sending a QR code and others pushing it straight into their app, and the [step-by-step install guide](/guides/how-to-install-esim/) covers both routes on iPhone and Android.

Then make two settings, in this order. Set the eSIM as the line for mobile data, and turn data roaming off on the home line. In that order, nothing can silently bill you at a roaming rate while you are still on the airport Wi-Fi.

That is also the moment to decide what happens to your normal number. A travel eSIM carries data only, so calls and texts to your home number keep running on the physical SIM, which is usually what you want, because bank codes keep arriving. If both lines should stay live, [dual SIM with an eSIM](/guides/dual-sim-and-esim/) explains the settings.

## Three things to check before you buy

**Does the phone take an eSIM?** Most handsets sold since 2019 do, but the list has exceptions by region and model. Our [compatibility check](/guides/esim-compatibility-check/) takes about 30 seconds and needs no download. Apple's own support page lists the [supported iPhone models](https://support.apple.com/en-us/109317) if you want the manufacturer's answer.

**Does the plan outlast the trip?** Validity is where cheap plans hide their limits. A 10GB plan with 3 days of validity is not a heavy week, whatever it costs. Match the validity window to the length of the stay first, then compare prices inside that group.

**Are you going unlimited for the right reason?** An unlimited plan is worth the premium when you cannot predict your usage. For a New York week, the metered 5GB plan at $5.00 covers normal use, and the cheapest 30-day unlimited tier at $49.99 buys a lot of certainty for a lot of money.

Get those three right and the buying decision is small. Pick a bucket that covers the stay, pick it on price rather than on coverage claims, and read the fair-use line if you decide to go unlimited.

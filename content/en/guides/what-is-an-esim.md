An eSIM is a SIM card built into your phone at the factory instead of shipped on a piece of plastic. A small chip is soldered to the motherboard, and when you "install" an eSIM you're downloading a carrier profile onto that chip — nothing is inserted, nothing is swapped, and no ejector pin is involved. For travelers, that one difference changes how you buy mobile data abroad: online, in advance, with every price visible before you commit.

## What does eSIM stand for and how does it work

"SIM" stands for Subscriber Identity Module — the credential that tells a mobile network "this line is mine, bill it to this account." A physical SIM stores that credential on a removable card. An eSIM (the "e" is *embedded*) stores it on a reprogrammable chip inside the device, and the profile it holds can be replaced over and over without touching the hardware.

When you buy a travel eSIM, the provider issues a profile tied to a package — say 10GB valid for 30 days in one country or region. You install it while still at home; it typically activates when it first registers on a partner network in the destination, not at the moment of installation.

## What actually gets installed on your phone

The "install" is a small configuration file, not a physical thing. Three details matter:

- **The profile is dormant until activation.** Installing a week before your flight is safe — the validity clock usually starts on first connection abroad, though a few providers start it at install, so check the plan's terms.
- **The QR code is mostly single-use.** A travel eSIM profile is bound to one device. Scanning it again on another phone fails by design; if you change devices you generally need the provider to reissue.
- **Deleting is free and final.** An expired or used-up profile can be deleted any time. Nothing about the phone's eSIM capability is consumed — you can install the next trip's profile immediately.

## How many eSIMs can a phone hold

A recent iPhone stores eight or more profiles; most Androids hold five or more. Only one is active for data at a time, and you switch between them in Settings in seconds — which is why multi-country trips work so well: land in [Japan](/compare/japan/), finish with its package, activate the [Thailand](/compare/thailand/) one on arrival, and never handle a physical card. ## eSIM vs physical SIM for international travel

- **Buy before you fly.** A physical local SIM means finding a shop after landing, at airport prices. An eSIM is bought from a sofa and works the moment you step off the plane.
- **Your home number stays alive.** The home SIM stays in its slot for calls and texts while the eSIM carries data — the two run side by side.
- **Prices are posted, not negotiated.** Travel eSIMs are sold online, so every provider's real prices can be compared before buying — which is exactly what our [country comparison pages](/compare/) do.
- **It can't be silently swapped.** A thief can't move an eSIM to another device without your credentials, which is why carriers increasingly use them for security. The flip side: you can't hand a used eSIM to a friend either.

The honest exceptions where plastic still wins: you need a local phone number with prepaid credit, your device predates eSIM support ([check yours in 30 seconds](/guides/esim-compatibility-check/)), or your phone is carrier-locked. ## How much does an eSIM cost

Less than most travelers expect — and the destination matters far more than the provider or the technology. The same gigabyte can cost several times more in one country than in its neighbor, purely because of local network competition. ## Do you get a phone number with a travel eSIM

Usually not a callable one. Travel eSIMs are data-first: some include a number for receiving SMS, a few include outbound calling credit, but none replace your home line for calls.

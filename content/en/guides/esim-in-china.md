China is the destination where the data plan decides whether you can buy anything at all. Payment runs through QR codes, a QR code is generated on the phone, and a phone with no connection is an empty wallet standing at the counter. A travel eSIM fixes that in the time it takes to make coffee, and it costs less than one airport taxi.

The question people actually arrive with is a different one. Travellers hear that China runs on a local phone number and go looking for one, which is why the number is the first thing worth settling. The operator of the country's largest wallet app answers it in writing, and the answer is that a visitor does not need a mainland number at all.

Everything below comes from two sources. Prices, validity windows and fair-use thresholds are calculated from our own plan database, which holds every prepaid plan sold into mainland China that we can find. The payment rules are quoted from the operators' published help pages and press releases, linked at the point they are used.

## Why a China eSIM is mostly a payments decision

Start with what is genuinely different here. In the USA the traveller's question is which network, and across most of Europe it is which price. Mainland China turns the data plan into a payment decision, because the retail economy assumes a phone that can scan.

Cities in China are largely cashless. Street stalls, temple ticket windows, metro gates and night-market noodle stands all present a QR code, and the transaction completes in the app. Cash remains legal tender and larger shops still accept cards, but the working assumption at the counter is the phone. A plan that fails on landing costs far more than the plan itself, in missed payments, queues and taxis paid in cash you did not bring.

There is a second reason the familiar comparison collapses. Every travel eSIM brand we track routes mainland traffic through the same three carriers, which the [China network report](/networks/china/#carriers) sets out carrier by carrier. Paying more for a particular network buys nothing here, exactly as it buys nothing in the USA. What is left to compare is price, validity and the fair-use line on the plan page.

## Nothing in China works without a live connection

A payment QR code is not an image stored on the phone. It is generated on demand, and generating it takes data. The same applies to the ride-hailing app, the translation tool, the restaurant menu behind the square on the table and the train ticket sitting in your wallet.

That produces a sequencing problem, and it is the most useful thing to plan around. Land without data and everything that would help you get data sits behind a connection: the provider's website, its app, the app store that hosts the app, and often the airport Wi-Fi login screen as well. Public networks in China commonly ask for a phone number before granting access, and no published rule settles whether that number has to be a mainland one, so the requirement varies venue by venue and cannot be relied on.

Installing the eSIM at home removes the problem rather than managing it. An eSIM is a profile the phone downloads instead of a card you insert, which is what allows it to be installed anywhere with a connection and then switched on when the plane lands. The [install walkthrough](/guides/how-to-install-esim/) covers both the QR route and the in-app route on iPhone and Android.

## Does a travel eSIM give you a Chinese phone number

No, and for this destination that turns out not to matter. A travel eSIM is a data-first product. Most plans in our file carry data only, a handful can receive an SMS, and none of them issues a mainland mobile number.

The number matters because of the wallet, and here the operator's own documentation is unusually direct. [Tencent's help centre](https://cs.help.wechat.com/hc/en-us/articles/11975770896271-How-to-Set-Up-International-Cards-for-Weixin-Pay) answers the question under its own heading: an international phone number is accepted as long as it can receive SMS verification codes, and identity is proved with a passport rather than with a number. The same page lists the documents a visitor can use, with the passport named first.

That answer reshapes the setup. Your existing number stays where it is, on the physical SIM, doing the one job that still matters, which is receiving verification codes. The eSIM carries the data. [Running both lines at once](/guides/dual-sim-and-esim/) is the standard configuration and needs a single setting: point mobile data at the eSIM, and leave data roaming switched off on the home line so it cannot connect on its own and bill you.

One habit is worth avoiding. Switching the home line off to save battery or roaming charges is the move that breaks the wallet, because a line that cannot receive a code cannot complete a setup.

## The three things an international card still cannot do

An international card handles ordinary spending, and the operator's pages are explicit about where it stops.

- **Red packets.** The cash gifts Chinese users exchange inside chats do not accept international cards.
- **Person-to-person transfers.** Paying an individual is treated differently from paying a merchant and is not supported.
- **Holding a balance.** Money comes off the linked card per transaction rather than being topped up into the wallet.

There is also a ceiling per transaction. Tencent publishes a single-transaction limit of 6,500 yuan, a monthly cumulative limit of 50,000 yuan and a yearly cumulative limit of 65,000 yuan for international cards. Those figures sit on a help page rather than in a news release, which makes them the version worth trusting.

None of the limits interferes with an ordinary trip. They matter for the two purchases that can be large, hotels and long-distance transport, and both are simpler to book on the operator's own site before flying, charged to the same card without a wallet in the middle.

## The 90 day window that drops the 3 percent fee

The fee structure is published and simple. Payments of 200 yuan or less carry no fee. Above that, 3 percent applies to the whole amount rather than to the portion over the threshold, so a 300 yuan payment costs 9 yuan while a 200 yuan payment costs nothing.

Tencent has added two things that change the arithmetic for a visitor. A first-time international card gets the 3 percent waived for 90 consecutive days from the first purchase, on daily spending up to 1,000 yuan, provided the card is bound before 31 December 2026. Separately, through 2026 the 200 yuan fee-free threshold stays open to every international card holder and not only to new ones, as the company [confirmed when it announced the inbound payment changes](https://www.tencent.com/index.php/en-us/articles/2202338.html).

For a traveller that produces a straightforward habit. Daily spending is mostly metro rides, taxis, convenience stores and casual meals, and it sits well under 200 yuan, which keeps it fee-free either way. The 3 percent lands on hotel bills, restaurant dinners and shopping, and that is where the 90 day window does real work on a first trip. Where a bill lands just above the threshold and the group is settling separately anyway, two smaller payments attract no fee at all.

PayPal users should know about one more route. Tencent and PayPal World have connected their systems so that a PayPal wallet can pay at Weixin Pay merchants by scanning, opening first for accounts based in the USA. For anyone who already holds that wallet it removes the app entirely from the trip.

## Sizing a plan around QR codes and ride hailing

Scanning does not use much data. The code itself is a few kilobytes. What uses data is everything around it: a map that redraws as you walk, a ride-hailing app tracking a car in real time, photo uploads, translation held up to a menu, and a video call home in the evening.

Two numbers set the scale before the table does. The cheapest single plan in our China file is 0.51 dollars for a 100MB one-day pack, which is a terrible match for a trip, and the best rate in the file is 0.48 dollars per gigabyte on a 50GB bucket with 30 days of validity. Between them sits the entire pricing structure of the market, and the lesson is that buying small is expensive.

| A few days of maps and messages | 1GB with 7 days of validity | $1.99 |
| A normal week | 5GB with 21 days of validity | $5.00 |
| A week with video and hotspot use | 10GB with 30 days of validity | $9.00 |
| Heavy use or a long stay | 50GB with 30 days of validity | $24.00, or $0.48 per GB |

All figures in this guide come from our own plan database and were last verified on 7 October 2026. The spread inside a single specification is the part worth staring at. The same 5GB allowance with a week or more of validity is sold at $5.00 by one brand and at $16.00 by another, so identical data carries a price difference of more than three times.

Then there is the unlimited question, which in China is where the fine print earns its keep. We read the fair-use policy behind every unlimited plan, and the published daily full-speed allowances are not close to each other:

- **Saily** publishes 5GB per day, then around 1 Mbps, the most generous published threshold we track.
- **Airalo, Jetpac and Roamic** publish 3GB per day, then 1 Mbps until the cycle resets.
- **Nomad** publishes 2GB per day, then 1 Mbps.
- **aloSIM** publishes 1.5GB to 2GB per day, then roughly 512 Kbps.
- **Yesim** states 500MB to 2GB per day depending on the destination, printed before purchase.
- **Ubigi** varies by plan with a wide spread, so the plan page is the only reliable source.
- **Holafly** publishes no daily figure and describes throttling as temporary, with independent testing in early 2026 putting the cut-off nearer 500MB to 1GB per day.
- **Roami** publishes 30GB per day, yet its own pages describe the throttle differently from one another, so treat the figure as destination-dependent.

For a trip built around payments, the meter does the job. 5GB covers a normal week for $5.00, while the cheapest unlimited plan that covers a full week is $15.00 and the cheapest unlimited single day is $3.00. Unlimited buys certainty about not running out, not speed, and the daily figure above is the number that tells you which of the two you are actually getting.

## When the itinerary adds Hong Kong or Macao

Hong Kong and Macao are part of China, and their mobile markets are not. A plan bought for the mainland does not carry across either border, and this is not a matter of reading the small print differently. It is how the products are built.

Not one of the brands we track sells a single plan that covers the mainland and Hong Kong together, or the mainland and Macao. Each is a separate product line with its own carriers underneath. Mainland plans ride China Mobile, China Unicom and China Telecom. Hong Kong plans ride CSL, 3HK and SmarTone. Macao plans ride CTM, 3 Macau and China Telecom. Those carrier lists are identical across every brand in each territory, so as everywhere else, the brand does not change the network.

A route that lands in Hong Kong and crosses into Shenzhen or Guangzhou therefore needs a second purchase. A Hong Kong plan of 3GB covering three days starts at $2.99, and a mainland week at 5GB starts at $5.00, which puts a two-stage itinerary at roughly $8 in data. Both profiles live on the phone at once, and one is switched on as the other stops, which is the same mechanic as any dual-line setup.

The alternative to buying twice is an Asia regional bundle, and it is worth pricing before dismissing. Regional products build their own country lists, and the [Asia coverage breakdown](/guides/best-asia-esim/) shows which destinations a given bundle actually includes, because mainland China, Hong Kong and Macao are commonly listed as separate line items rather than folded into one product. The two territories can also be compared directly on the [Hong Kong eSIM](/compare/hong-kong/) and [Macao eSIM](/compare/macao/) tables.

One device note applies to this corner of the map. Handsets sold inside mainland China cannot install an inbound eSIM profile, and Hong Kong units have historically shipped without eSIM support as well, so a phone bought in either market should be checked before any of this is bought. Apple's [list of iPhone models with eSIM support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/109317) covers the hardware, and the [compatibility check](/guides/esim-compatibility-check/) settles it in about 30 seconds.

## The work that has to happen on your own wifi

Everything in this guide that can fail, fails for the same reason, which is that it was left until arrival. Three tasks belong at home, on a connection you control.

**Install the profile before departure.** An eSIM has to be downloaded, and inside the mainland the sites and stores that would serve it can be out of reach. Install at home, then switch the line on after landing.

**Set the two lines in the right order.** Point mobile data at the eSIM first, then confirm that data roaming is off on the home line. In that order nothing can quietly connect at a roaming rate while you are still on the airport Wi-Fi.

**Keep the home number reachable.** The wallet verification code arrives by SMS on your own number, so the physical SIM stays live. [Dual SIM with an eSIM](/guides/dual-sim-and-esim/) covers the settings when both lines need to stay on at once.

Get those three right and the rest is a price decision.
